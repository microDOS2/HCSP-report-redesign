The field, and the gap it leaves open

§4 · The field

A forty-year graveyard, and four organizations still standing

Commercial SCWO has a long casualty list built on corrosion and salt plugging — which is precisely why the survivors' operating records, and their finances, carry the evidentiary weight.

As of 2021 only about six commercial SCWO plants operated worldwide; a 2019 review found all commercially constructed plants except two had closed — mainly on corrosion, plugging, and economics. The flow below follows the field from the 1980s pioneers to today's four verifiable commercial operators. Dashed spans mark entities whose dates are graded estimates rather than documented; the ✕ endpoints are the lesson.

Only four organizations operate verifiable commercial-scale SCWO today. 374Water (NASDAQ: SCWO), a Duke University spinout and the closest analogue to the HCSP, sells the containerized AirSCWO 6 — yet its FY2025 Form 10-K shows revenue of $215,037 against a $21.0M net loss. General Atomics fields PERSES, the incumbent: commercial since ~2012, more than 6 million gallons destroyed, EPA-verified, but with a mixed reliability record. Aquarden (Denmark) sells the all-electric SuperOx — the field's all-electric benchmark. Revive Environmental operates Battelle's PFAS Annihilator as North America's first fully permitted commercial-scale SCWO PFAS destruction service.

The incineration incumbency is the real competitor. Roughly 24 permitted US RCRA combustors run at 87–92% utilization with one new unit permitted in three decades and 5–7%/yr price escalation. HCSP does not need to beat their price by a little; the no-air-permit moat and on-site liability elimination let it sell speed and certainty while pricing at $2–5/gal beneath a $5–50/gal umbrella.

§4B · The field, up close

Four survivors, one shared blind spot: everything below a million dollars

Read the filings and registries, not the websites: a going-concern listed pure-play, a niche line inside a $3.2B defense prime, a negative-equity Danish shop of eleven people, and a centralized service JV that just changed CEOs. None of them sells anything a small facility can buy and run itself.

The four profiles below are built only from primary records — SEC filings and prospectuses, the Danish CVR registry, state procurement and settlement documents, EPA and DIU/ESTCP program records — each dated and drillable. For each operator: the balance sheet as filed, the customers that actually pay versus the pipeline that is claimed, the smallest thing they will sell or do, who they are built to serve, and where that leaves the low end of the market.

The cross-company read

Put side by side, the four survivors are financially nothing alike — going concern, fortress, insolvent-on-equity, patient JV — yet their commercial floors cluster in the same place. Every cell drills to its dated source.

The scale gap, drawn

Each lane starts where that competitor's smallest evidenced offering begins, on a deal-size axis read against facility size — small farm or fabrication shop at the left, national programs at the right. Dashed or hollow spans are services and pilots, not equipment a customer can buy and run. The blue zone below ≈$1M is what nobody serves: that is HCSP's opening.
